Street networks

Begin with the circulation system: the routes that organize movement into, through, and around each neighborhood.

1 · Street network2 · Land use3 · Bars

Look for: dense versus sparse local street networks, major arterials cutting through neighborhoods, freeway barriers, multiple versus limited routes in and out, and differences in connectivity.

Interpret the hierarchy: local streets organize movement within neighborhoods, while larger roads can increase accessibility and traffic but may also divide residential areas or channel activity into particular corridors.

Add land use

Now place activity-generating nonresidential buildings onto the same street networks. Residential building footprints are intentionally omitted so the map emphasizes destinations and institutional settings.

Look for: commercial corridors, industrial districts, government and educational clusters, mixed activity areas, and places where land uses line up with major roads or remain embedded in local street networks.

Use cautiously: these building classifications are derived from OpenStreetMap and should be treated as a spatial inventory for comparison rather than a complete census of every establishment or building use.

Add bars

Finally, add drinking establishments to the street-network and land-use context. Use their locations to think about where people converge, how those places are accessed, and what other activity settings surround them.

1 · Street network2 · Land use3 · Bars

Look for: concentrations versus isolated establishments, placement along major corridors versus local streets, proximity to commercial or institutional activity, and differences in the density of drinking establishments between the two neighborhoods.

Click a point to see the OpenStreetMap name and establishment type. The layer includes bars, pubs, nightclubs, and biergartens and should not be treated as a complete inventory of alcohol-serving businesses.
