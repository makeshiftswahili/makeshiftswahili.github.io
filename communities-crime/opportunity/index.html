Street networks

Begin with the street network: the routes that organize movement into, through, and around each neighborhood, shaping the flow of ambient populations. Note that your neighborhood boundary may overlap with important features like freeways or major arterials.

1 · Street network2 · Land use3 · Bars

Look for: dense versus sparse local street networks, major arterials cutting through neighborhoods, freeway barriers, multiple versus limited routes in and out, and differences in connectivity.

Interpret the hierarchy: local streets organize movement within neighborhoods, while larger roads can increase accessibility and traffic but may also divide residential areas or channel activity into particular corridors. Consider how street networks shape the activity spaces of local residents vs. visitors, and the awareness spaces of potential offenders.

Add land use

Now place activity-generating nonresidential buildings onto the same street networks. Residential building footprints are intentionally omitted so the map emphasizes destinations and institutional settings.

1 · Street network2 · Land use3 · Bars

Look for: commercial corridors, industrial districts, government and educational clusters, mixed activity areas, and places where land uses line up with major roads or remain embedded in local street networks. Explore your neighborhood at the micro-level, zooming in to make note of where edges (sharp changes in land use) exist. Note that residential buildings are omitted from the maps; toggle between the street map and satellite imagery to get a better sense of the built environment.

Use cautiously: these building classifications are derived from OpenStreetMap and should be treated as a spatial inventory for comparison rather than a complete census of every establishment or building use.

Add bars

Finally, add drinking establishments to the street-network and land-use context. Use their locations to think about where people converge, how those places are accessed, and what other activity settings surround them.

1 · Street network2 · Land use3 · Bars

Look for: concentrations versus isolated establishments, placement along major corridors versus local streets, proximity to commercial or institutional activity, and differences in the density of drinking establishments between the two neighborhoods.

Click a point to see the OpenStreetMap name and establishment type. The layer includes bars, pubs, nightclubs, and biergartens and should not be treated as a complete inventory of alcohol-serving businesses.

Identify a crime-opportunity micro-place

Use the two maps below to zoom in to one micro-place per neighborhood where you think crime opportunities are more likely to concentrate—that is, where spatiotemporal convergence between motivated offenders and suitable targets is more likely to occur in the absence of capable guardians.

Think in space and time: in addition to where these conditions may converge, consider the times of day when motivated offenders, suitable targets, and the absence of capable guardians are most likely to come together.

Choose a micro-place: zoom and pan each map until it is centered on the specific place you think offers the strongest opportunity structure. Use the street network, land uses, bars, and—when useful—satellite imagery as evidence for your choice.
